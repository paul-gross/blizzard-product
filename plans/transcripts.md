# Plan — `epic:transcripts`

A fleet that learns from its own conversations first has to be able to reach them. Today a worker's conversation lives and dies as files on its own runner — the harness's own JSONL, read in place by the runner's panel, retained entirely at the harness's mercy. That is workable for one runner and hopeless for twenty. This epic gives every conversation a durable home at the hub: runners normalize and stream what their workers say, the hub keeps it, and an operator can open any chunk on the board and read exactly what happened — the conversation, the tool calls, the thinking, the subagents. It is the raw material `epic:analytics` counts and the retrospective sweeps of `epic:self-sourced-work` mine: a fleet can only learn from what it can reach.

## What already holds

The runner already reads transcripts — a deliberately read-only feature (locator, parser, viewer) over the harness's files — and already owns session identity: every worker session id is runner-minted and recorded per lease and per spawn, which is what makes a worker's transcript findable at all. Nothing in blizzard writes a transcript today, and nothing retains one.

The runner→hub fact lane is the delivery template: a store-and-forward outbound buffer with a per-runner sequence and a hub-side high-water mark — at-least-once delivery made exactly-once by hub idempotence, buffered through outages and flushed on return.

On the reading side, the board's chunk detail page is already transition-keyed and tabbed, and the board and runner panel are one web codebase with two thin apps — a transcript viewer is a shared component, not two implementations.

Runaway sessions are already bounded upstream: session rotation caps a live transcript's size and mints a fresh session when it overflows.

## The shape of the data

**Measured sizes** (≈2,000 real sessions on the founding runner): median ≈410 KB, p90 ≈1.3 MB, p99 ≈3.3 MB, max ≈5.3 MB. Every cap and storage estimate below stands on these numbers.

**The segment is the unit** — of shipping, of storage, and of reading. A segment is a contiguous run of turns belonging to one (chunk, transition, spawn generation): the runner stamps a new segment boundary at every spawn, every resume, and every rotation, all of which it initiates and therefore always sees. Segments are self-describing — segment id, turn range, and an explicit final marker — because the transcript lane is ordered only against itself: a segment's tail may arrive after the step's completion has already landed on the fact lane, so the hub never infers a segment's completeness from a transition; it waits for the marker and tolerates late tails. In the UI, segmentation is what makes resume legible: the second visit to a build node shows only the turns after the resume, opening with a link to the segment it continued from.

**Normalized turns are the only thing on the wire.** The runner ships blizzard's turn model, never harness bytes: environment and user turns, assistant text, tool calls, tool results, thinking, and sidechain subagent conversations as nested turns of their own. Tool calls stay structured — tool name plus input as data, never flattened into prose — because that structure is the contract `epic:analytics` queries against. Every segment carries a version stamp: the normalizer version and the harness version that produced it, so a better future parser can coexist with old rows and "what was this worker running" stays answerable. The raw file stays runner-local as a fidelity backstop, at the harness's mercy exactly as today.

## What to build

- **A per-harness transcript source behind the adapter seam.** The harness-specific knowledge — where transcripts live, what their records mean — moves behind the harness adapter seam as a single operation: normalized turns since a given position. Today that knowledge is Claude-Code-specific and sits outside the seam; moving it in is what lets `epic:adapters`' breadth slice bring Codex and OpenCode workers whose conversations centralize the same way.
- **The transcript lane.** A second outbound buffer with its own sequence and hub high-water mark, so bulk never queues ahead of truth: a fat conversation delta must never delay a completion or a gate decision. Flush is opportunistic with a per-record cap; the lane's only promise is that a step's segments are complete, with final markers shipped, by step close.
- **The hub store.** Segment rows in the hub's database, content compressed at rest, portable across the sqlite and postgres arms. Reads are lazy and per-segment — transcript content is never inlined into a chunk detail payload. Retention is deliberate non-machinery: keep everything, compressed, and revisit at first pain — at the caps below a busy night adds tens of megabytes, which is years of runway, with the hub's backup weight growing in proportion.
- **Caps at both ends.** The chunk transcript budget is 64 MB — fifty p90 sessions' worth of conversation, a generous ceiling for honest work and a hard wall for a rogue. The runner enforces it as the well-behaved case: it stops shipping and marks the segment truncated. The hub enforces it independently as the rogue case: over-budget or oversized records are rejected but acknowledged, so a misbehaving runner wastes its own budget without wedging its lane. A per-runner daily rate of 2 GB backs both as a coarse guard — roughly thirty busy nights' worth in one day, meaningless to honest work and a wall to a flood. Truncation is always visible in the UI, never silent.
- **Operator-only access.** A transcript holds everything a worker saw — file contents, environment output, whatever a tool happened to print — and centralizing that raises its stakes from one machine to the network. Reading transcripts requires the operator role; a viewer sees the fleet's state, not its conversations. (The exposure itself shrinks when `epic:security`'s worker-lockdown scopes what a worker holds in the first place.)
- **The hub transcript view.** Chunk detail grows a transcript surface organized by the node history it already renders: pick a step, read its segments. Thinking renders collapsed by default; sidechain conversations read both ways — nested in place within the parent's flow, and openable alone as a conversation in their own right; resume seams show as continued-from links; truncation shows as itself. Built as the shared viewer component the runner panel already uses, extended with the turn kinds the current one drops.
- **The runner panel rule: local until acked, hub after.** An active lease — or any segment not yet acknowledged by the hub — reads live from the harness's own file, exactly as today: real-time, and standing even when the hub is away, which is precisely when an operator is staring at a worker. A closed lease reads from the hub, fetched outbound by the runner and served to its panel. Once a segment is acked, the harness may purge its file freely and nothing is lost. The accepted cost: during a hub outage, closed-lease transcripts are unreachable from the runner panel — consistent with the eventual-reachability contract, and the live view is the one an outage actually needs.
- **Best-effort backfill.** `blizzard runner transcript backfill`: walk the runner's own lease and spawn records — never sweep the harness directory, which on a working machine is mostly the operator's own sessions — normalize whatever is still on disk, and ship it deduplicated by session id. Idempotent and rerunnable, closing with a report of what imported and what was already gone. Accepted imperfections, stated up front: files the harness rotated away are lost, and pre-epic sessions recorded no resume offsets, so an in-place-resumed session backfills as one merged segment rather than splitting at its seams.

## What this epic is not

Not a raw archive: harness bytes never cross the wire, and re-normalization of history is the backfill verb's job, not the hub's. Not live at the hub: the hub's view is honest as of the last flush, and real-time belongs to the runner panel. Not stdout: the runner's worker stdout/stderr captures stay local — shipping them, perhaps only on failure, is a later question. Not retention machinery: nothing prunes until keeping everything first hurts.

## Open questions

None — the caps carry their numbers above, and whether thinking turns are worth indexing belongs to `epic:analytics`, whose plan carries it.
